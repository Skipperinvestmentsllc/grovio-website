Am I Doing Enough Homeschooling?

A letter titled Am I Doing Enough?

Published August 4, 2026 · 3:31
Listen on SpotifyApple Podcasts →Amazon Music →iHeartRadio →

A short answer

A homeschool day does not have to match an invisible checklist to count. In this letter, Claire looks at the way a parent can replay the day and see only the unfinished writing lesson, missed math set, or chapter left unread. Yet her child is carrying a kitchen-table conversation about why leaves change color, a moment that never made the list. The question is not whether every planned task was completed. It is whether something landed, sparked curiosity, and helped your child keep moving. This reflection offers a gentler way to notice learning without pretending that tiredness and uncertainty disappear.

Written and narrated by Claire, the voice behind the grovio Guide.

The original letter

I remember sitting on the couch after he went to bed one night and trying to replay the day in my head. I don't know why I do this to myself, but I do it almost every night. I sit there in the quiet and I try to account for the hours, and somehow my brain just skips right over everything that actually happened and lands immediately on the things we didn't finish. And before I even realize what's happening, I'm quietly building a case against myself.

We didn't get to the writing lesson. We ran out of time for the second math problem set. I meant to read another chapter and we didn't. The list of what we didn't do somehow grows louder than everything we did, and by the time I've finished replaying the day, I'm sitting there in my living room genuinely wondering if I am failing my child.

I don't know if this is just me. But I have a feeling it's not.

What your child may remember from the day

The thing I've noticed is that he never seems to be doing the same accounting I am. So I'll ask him at dinner what his favorite part of the day was. Half expecting him to say nothing, or to look at me blankly.

And he'll say something like: "Mom. Do you remember when we figured out why leaves change color? I told Fisher about that today."

Something we talked about at the kitchen table. Something I had forgotten entirely. Something that didn't make it onto any checklist.

He's not counting what we didn't finish. He's carrying what actually landed.

And I'm sitting there with my invisible checklist wondering if we did enough.

When I replay the day and go straight to the unfinished things, I'm not measuring learning. I'm measuring compliance. How closely we stuck to a plan that was never really about him. It was about me feeling like I had proof. Proof that I did my job. Proof that today counted.

Learning is not the same as checking every box

I would put him to bed and then just sit there. And sometimes I would cry, if I'm being honest. Not a dramatic cry. Just the quiet kind where you're not even sure what you're crying about except that you're tired and scared and not sure if you're enough for this.

You can text a friend and she'll say you're doing great. You'll feel better for about forty-five minutes. Then the feeling comes back. Because the reassurance was borrowed. It wasn't built on anything you could actually see.

I eventually found something that helped me see it.

But that's a different letter.

*

A question I've been sitting with: What if the things I'm not counting are the things that actually mattered most today?

Love,

Claire

Questions this letter helps hold

What if we did not finish everything today?
A day can still hold learning when written tasks remain. Claire's letter does not promise every plan will fit; it asks you to notice what did land instead of counting only what did not.
How can I stop needing constant reassurance about homeschool?
The letter names how borrowed reassurance can fade quickly. Noticing concrete moments your child carries forward can give you something more durable to return to.
