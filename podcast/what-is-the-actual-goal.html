Episode 6 transcript What Is the Actual Goal?

What is the actual goal behind the routines in our homeschool?

Claire asks what your homeschool routines are actually for, and how returning to the real goal can loosen the grip of needless conflict and performance.

Direct answer

A short answer grounded in the transcript

The actual goal is the learning, communication, curiosity, and relationship you want to support—not a ritual that only makes school look right. Ask what each practice is for, then keep the parts that move your child toward that purpose.

Section 1

The question that cuts through the surface answer

Claire describes asking what the actual goal is as a useful interruption. It pushes past the first explanation and reveals whether a demand serves learning or simply reflects a reason you have never examined.

Section 2

When handwriting practice stops serving communication

A disagreement about letters, lines, and slant made the distinction concrete. Neat handwriting was only valuable because it could help her son communicate clearly; insisting on a particular look was moving him toward hating the work instead.

Section 3

A goal is different from a ritual

Read-alouds, kitchen-counter math, and library trips survived the question because their purpose was clear. Repeating worksheets he had already mastered or enforcing a school-like format did not. When the reason is only that it looks right, Claire calls it a ritual rather than a goal.

FAQ

Questions this episode helps answer

How can I find the real goal of a homeschool activity?

Name the immediate reason, then ask why that reason matters and whether the activity is moving your child toward it. Keep following the answer until you reach a purpose you can clearly defend.

What should I do when a routine no longer serves its purpose?

Loosen or change the routine. If it is creating conflict without supporting learning, communication, curiosity, or relationship, the routine may be acting as a ritual instead of helping with the goal.

What Is the Actual Goal?

Four words. That's all.

Four words that I have found more disruptive and more useful than almost anything else in four years of homeschooling.

What is the actual goal?

It's not a comfortable question. It doesn't confirm what you're already doing — it interrogates it. And when you start asking it honestly, about everything, you find out very quickly how many things you are doing for reasons you cannot clearly articulate.

We were arguing about handwriting once. The letters weren't sitting on the line. The slant was wrong. I could hear my own voice getting that particular tone — the one that means I've stopped teaching and started just insisting.

He looked up at me and said: "Why does it matter how it looks if you can read it?"

I didn't have a good answer.

So I asked myself: what is the actual goal here?

The first answer was neat handwriting. Then I pushed past it. Why neat handwriting? So he can communicate clearly in writing. And is what we're doing right now — this argument, this insistence on the slant — moving us toward that?

It was not. It was moving us toward him hating handwriting.

That's what the question does. It cuts through the surface answer to the real one.

Some things survive it easily. The read-aloud survives it. The math at the kitchen counter survives it. The library trips survive it. When I ask what the actual goal is, I can answer immediately and we keep going.

But a lot of things don't survive it. Worksheets of problems he already knows how to do — what is the actual goal? I have never come up with a good answer. Insisting on a format for a subject he's already mastered — what's the actual goal? Usually: so it looks right. So it matches some mental image of what school is supposed to look like. So I feel like I've done my job.

That's not a goal. That's a ritual.
