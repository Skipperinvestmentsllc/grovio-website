I made a schedule.

Not just a loose idea of how the day might go — a schedule. Color coded. Time-blocked in thirty-minute increments starting at eight o'clock in the morning. Math first, then reading, then a fifteen-minute break, then writing, then lunch at noon exactly, then science, then history, then what I had optimistically labeled enrichment, which in retrospect I'm not sure I had any plan for at all. I found it recently while cleaning and I stood there looking at it for a long moment trying to remember who made this and whether she was okay.

She was fine. But she was operating under a very specific misunderstanding about what homeschooling was.

By nine fifteen on the first real day, we were off schedule. I know it was nine fifteen because I remember looking at the clock and doing a kind of internal calculation about how recoverable this was. We had gotten through some math — and then he had a question about something tangentially related and looked at me with that particular expression that meant his brain had just caught fire about something.

"But wait — why does that actually work that way?"

Twenty minutes later we were still there. I couldn't bring myself to cut it off. And suddenly we were behind, and I spent the rest of the morning trying to get back to a schedule that had already stopped reflecting our day.

We never really got back on schedule. Not that day and not most days after it.

School is structured the way it is because it has to manage twenty-five children, seven hours a day, with one teacher and a legal obligation to cover specific material by a specific date. The schedule exists because of those constraints.

I had none of those constraints.

I had one child, a dining room table, and complete freedom to do whatever actually worked.

And I was voluntarily recreating the constraints.

I think it was fear dressed up as rigor. The schedule felt like proof — proof that we were doing school, real school, the kind that counts. Without it I wasn't sure what we were doing or whether it was enough, and the structure gave me something to point to.

We are doing math from eight to eight thirty. See? School.

But my son doesn't learn in thirty-minute increments. He never has. He goes deep or he doesn't go at all. And cutting off a line of genuine curiosity because the clock said it was time for the next subject was probably the single most counterproductive thing I did that first year.

Fear dressed up as rigor.

That's what the schedule was.

A question I've been sitting with: What would our day look like if I designed it around how he actually learns, instead of how school taught me learning is supposed to look?
